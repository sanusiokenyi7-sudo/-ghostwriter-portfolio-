AI & Strategy

AI Is Not Your Competitive Advantage. The Decisions You Make With It Are.

AI is becoming easier to access. The harder competitive advantage is knowing what to do with it.

That distinction matters because technology can accelerate almost anything — including bad decisions.

A company can use AI to produce reports faster, write more content, analyse more information and automate repetitive work. But if the underlying questions are weak, the organisation may simply become faster at moving in the wrong direction.

The real question is no longer whether a business can use AI. Most can. The more interesting question is whether the business has redesigned the way it makes decisions because AI exists.

Information is becoming cheaper. Judgment is not.

AI can surface patterns, compare options and reduce the time required to process information. But someone still has to decide what matters, what evidence is reliable, what assumptions are wrong and what action should follow.

That is where competitive advantage begins to move.

It may come from asking better questions. It may come from having better internal data. It may come from understanding customers more deeply than competitors do. It may come from having processes that allow useful information to reach the person making the decision before the opportunity disappears.

Consider procurement. An organisation may have access to powerful forecasting and analytics tools. But if supplier risk information is fragmented, market signals arrive late and procurement decisions are based mainly on historical assumptions, the technology has not solved the real problem.

The advantage may not be who has the best AI. It may be who makes the best decisions with the same AI everyone else can access.

Operations

Your Supply Chain Isn't Slow. Your Decisions Are.

Supply chains are often described as unpredictable.

Sometimes they are. But many organisations discover their problems late rather than experiencing them suddenly.

A supplier does not necessarily become risky overnight. A transport route does not suddenly become inefficient without signals. A market does not usually change without warning.

The signals often exist somewhere.

The problem is that they may be sitting with suppliers, drivers, buyers, market participants or operational teams without reaching the person who needs them early enough.

This creates a dangerous illusion: the supply chain appears slow when the real delay happened in the decision process.

A supplier may still look attractive on price while transport costs are increasing. A vendor may still meet yesterday's performance standards while capacity is becoming constrained. A route may still appear commercially viable while the conditions around it are changing.

Procurement therefore cannot be reduced to finding the cheapest acceptable supplier.

The strategic question is increasingly about continuity: how early can the organisation recognise a problem, and how quickly can it respond?

Technology can improve visibility, but only after the organisation understands what it needs to see.

Sometimes the supply chain isn't slow. The decision simply arrived late.

GTM & B2B Growth

Most Companies Don't Have a Lead Problem. They Have a Trust Problem.

A company can have traffic, leads and a healthy-looking pipeline while deals still refuse to move.

That is because attention and confidence are different things.

Buyers can discover a company quickly. They can read the website, download the report and attend the webinar. None of that automatically means they believe the company understands their problem.

B2B buying has become increasingly complicated. The person consuming the content may not be the person signing the contract.

Finance may ask a different question from procurement. Operations may have a different concern from the executive sponsor. Legal may introduce another layer of uncertainty.

This is why generic content struggles.

A summary of industry trends may generate impressions. A useful point of view can create confidence.

Thought leadership becomes valuable when it helps a buyer understand something they were already struggling to articulate.

The strongest content doesn't simply say, "Here is what is happening."

It says, "Here is what this means for the decision you are trying to make."

Attention gets you noticed. Trust gets you considered.

Product & AI

The AI Feature Nobody Asked For Is Usually the Feature That Gets Built.

AI has made one part of product development increasingly exciting: building things is getting easier.

But when building becomes cheaper, the cost of building the wrong thing becomes easier to hide.

Teams can prototype an AI feature quickly. They can generate an interface, connect a model and demonstrate something impressive before the original problem has been properly understood.

The question changes from "Can we build it?" to "Should the customer care?"

The best AI feature may not be the most visible one.

It may be something that removes a frustrating task, reduces cognitive effort or quietly helps a customer reach an outcome faster.

Customers do not buy intelligence for its own sake. They buy progress.

This is why customer curiosity remains important even in an age of rapid technical experimentation.

Before building another AI capability, product teams should ask what problem disappears for the customer if the feature succeeds.

When building gets cheaper, judgment becomes more valuable.

Executive Strategy

The Most Expensive Business Decisions Are Often the Ones Nobody Thinks They Made.

Not every expensive decision appears in a meeting.

Some happen through inaction.

A process continues because it has always existed. A supplier remains because changing suppliers feels difficult. A product direction survives because too much work has already gone into it.

Eventually, the organisation is living with a decision that nobody remembers consciously making.

Familiarity can disguise risk.

This is where sunk-cost thinking becomes dangerous. The amount already invested in something may explain why people hesitate to change it, but it does not tell them what they should do next.

A better question is forward-looking:

If we were making this decision today, knowing what we know now, would we choose the same direction?

The answer does not automatically mean the original decision was wrong. It simply forces the organization to separate what still makes sense from what is being protected because it is familiar.

Good decision-making is not about avoiding every mistake. It is about creating enough clarity to recognize when a decision needs to change.

The organizations that do this well are not necessarily the ones that make perfect decisions. They are the ones willing to question decisions before those decisions become expensive habits.

Key question: What decision has your organization continued making simply because nobody has stopped to question it?

AI & STRATEGY

AI Is Not Your Competitive Advantage. The Decisions You Make With It Are.

AI is becoming easier to access. The harder competitive advantage is knowing what to do with it.

Give two companies access to the same AI tools and they can still produce very different results. One uses AI to accelerate work that already makes sense. The other uses it to accelerate decisions that should have been questioned first.

That distinction matters.

Information is becoming cheaper. Judgment is not.

The organizations that create value from AI will not simply be the ones using the most tools. They will be the ones asking better questions before those tools are used.

In procurement, for example, AI can compare suppliers, identify patterns and process large amounts of information quickly. But it cannot remove the need to understand supplier reliability, market conditions, operational risk or the consequences of a poor decision.

AI can make a bad process faster. It can also make a good process dramatically better.

The competitive advantage is knowing the difference.

OPERATIONS

Your Supply Chain Isn't Slow. Your Decisions Are.

Supply chain problems rarely appear out of nowhere.

A supplier misses a pattern before missing a delivery. A route becomes less reliable before it becomes a crisis. Prices move before procurement teams feel the full impact.

The information usually exists somewhere.

The problem is that organizations often discover it too late.

Visibility is therefore more than a reporting function. It creates decision time.

The earlier a business can see a change, the more options it has. It can negotiate, switch suppliers, adjust inventory, change routes or redesign the process before the problem becomes expensive.

Procurement sits directly inside this decision chain.

It is not simply about finding a supplier and getting a price. It is about understanding what could interrupt the business and acting before that interruption becomes unavoidable.

A faster supply chain is useful. A supply chain that gives decision-makers more time is even more valuable.

GTM & B2B GROWTH

Most Companies Don't Have a Lead Problem. They Have a Trust Problem.

Getting attention is easier than getting confidence.

A company can generate traffic, collect leads and publish content every week while potential customers still hesitate to buy.

The reason is simple: B2B decisions carry risk.

Buyers are not only asking whether a product works. They are asking whether the company understands their problem, whether the solution will survive implementation and whether choosing it will create another problem six months later.

That is where useful thought leadership matters.

Good thought leadership does not simply announce expertise. It demonstrates how the company thinks.

It gives the reader a reason to believe that the people behind the product understand the problem deeply enough to solve it.

Attention gets you noticed. Trust gets you considered.
